Spaghetti alla Carbonara
(Carbonara Tradizionale Romana)

★★★★★ 4.9 (940 reviews)

The immortal Roman masterpiece: bronze-die spaghetti tossed with sizzling cured guanciale, pecorino romano cheese, golden egg yolks, and toasted black pepper. No cream, pure culinary alchemy.

◉ Prep 10 min ◉ Cook 15 min ◉ Yield 4 serv ◉ 520 kcal

Ingredients

  • 400 g bronze-die Spaghetti or Rigatoni
  • 200 g cured Guanciale (pork jowl), cut into thick lardons
  • 4 fresh egg yolks + 1 whole egg
  • 100 g finely grated Pecorino Romano DOP
  • 30 g finely grated Parmigiano Reggiano
  • Freshly cracked black peppercorns (toasted)
  • Coarse sea salt for pasta water

Instructions

  1. Boil spaghetti in salted water until 2 minutes before al dente.
  2. Cook guanciale lardons in a cold skillet over medium-low heat until crispy outside and rendering clear golden fat (8-10 mins). Remove from heat.
  3. In a bowl, whisk egg yolks, whole egg, grated Pecorino, Parmigiano, and toasted black pepper into a rich cream.
  4. Transfer al dente pasta into skillet with guanciale fat and 2 tbsp pasta water; toss to coat.
  5. Allow skillet to cool 30 seconds off heat. Pour in egg-cheese mixture, tossing vigorously to form a silky, velvety emulsion without scrambling.
  6. Serve immediately crowned with crispy guanciale, extra Pecorino, and fresh pepper.
Off-Heat Emulsion

Never add eggs over a live burner; residual pasta heat creates the velvety cream.

Pasta alla Gricia

Guanciale, Pecorino, and black pepper without eggs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is cream used?⌄
Never in traditional Roman cooking; creaminess is purely an emulsion of starch, fat, cheese, and yolk.

About Italian Cuisine & Gastronomy

From ancient Roman hearths to Renaissance feasts: the art of terroir and pure ingredients.

Introduction to Italian Food

Italian cuisine is a magnificent tapestry woven from 20 distinct regions, each fiercely proud of its microclimate, dialect, olive oils, and heritage recipes passed down across centuries.

At its philosophical core, Italian cooking is not about complicated techniques or heavy sauces; it is the art of ingredient worship—sourcing sun-ripened tomatoes, raw-milk cheeses, and bronze-die wheat, letting pristine simplicity shine on the plate.

What Is Cucina Italiana?

A culinary culture rooted in hyper-seasonal produce, strict PDO (DOP) geographical protections, and the conviviality of the family table.

01. The Rule of Simplicity

Rarely more than 4 to 6 ingredients per dish, each of exceptional pristine quality.

02. Terroir & DOP Origin

Parmigiano from Parma, San Marzano from Mount Vesuvius, Prosciutto from San Daniele.

03. Al Dente Discipline

Pasta cooked with firm tooth-feel to preserve digestible starch structure and mouthfeel.

04. The Sacred Table Ritual

Meals are an unhurried social communion of antipasto, primo, secondo, and espresso.

Popular Traditional Italian Dishes

Timeless masterpieces celebrated across the trattorias, osterias, and family tables of Italy.

Spaghetti alla Carbonara (Carbonara Tradizionale)

Bronze-die spaghetti coated in a glossy emulsion of egg yolks, crispy guanciale fat, pecorino romano, and toasted black pepper.

Cultural Note: Rome’s immortal culinary gift, strictly prepared without cream, peas, or garlic.

Neapolitan Pizza Margherita (Pizza Napoletana STG)

Blistered, leopard-crusted wood-fired dough topped with crushed San Marzano tomatoes, buffalo mozzarella, and fresh sweet basil.

Cultural Note: UNESCO World Intangible Cultural Heritage honoring Queen Margherita of Savoy in 1889.

Wild Porcini Mushroom Risotto (Risotto ai Porcini)

Carnaroli rice toasted in shallot butter, slowly infused with rich porcini broth, finished with Parmigiano mantecatura.

Cultural Note: Northern Italy’s benchmark of culinary wave (all’onda) technique.

Lasagna alla Bolognese (Lasagne al Forno Emiliane)

Spinach egg pasta layered with 3-hour slow-cooked beef and pork ragù, nutmeg béchamel, and Parmigiano Reggiano.

Cultural Note: The Sunday family feast centerpiece of Emilia-Romagna.

Osso Buco alla Milanese (Ossobuco con Gremolada)

Cross-cut veal shanks braised in white wine and aromatic vegetables, crowned with citrusy lemon-parsley-garlic gremolata.

Cultural Note: Lombardy’s celebrated winter braise showcasing rich bone marrow.

Italian Coffee & Dessert Rituals

In Italy, dessert (dolce) is an elevated daily pleasure, invariably punctuated by a steaming shot of stovetop Moka or espresso.

"Never order a cappuccino after 11:00 AM; after meals, Italians strictly sip un caffè (espresso) or an amaro digestivo to honor digestion."

Popular Regional Treats & Specialties

Venetian Tiramisù (Tiramisù di Treviso)

Created in the Le Beccherie restaurant in Treviso, translating poetically to "pick me up" with espresso and silky mascarpone sabayon.

Piedmontese Panna Cotta (Panna Cotta ai Frutti di Bosco)

A delicate Piedmontese cooked cream gently set with gelatin, showcasing vanilla pods and wild forest berry compote.

Crispy Sicilian Cannoli (Cannoli di Ricotta di Pecora)

Crunchy fried wine pastry tubes filled to order with sweetened sheep’s milk ricotta and candied orange peel.

Key Italian Ingredients

🫒 Extra Virgin Olive Oil (Olio Extra Vergine di Oliva)

Cold-pressed fruity green oil from centuries-old groves in Tuscany, Puglia, and Liguria.

Use: Finishing raw on hot pastas, soups, bruschetta, and salads.
🧀 Parmigiano Reggiano DOP (Il Re dei Formaggi)

Raw cow’s milk cheese aged 24-36 months into crystalline umami-rich wheels.

Use: Grated over pastas, shaved onto carpaccio, or eaten in chunks with aged balsamic.
🍅 San Marzano Tomatoes (Pomodoro San Marzano dell’Agro Sarnese)

Plum tomatoes grown in volcanic soil near Mount Vesuvius with sweet flesh and low acidity.

Use: The foundational base of pizza sauce and simmered ragùs.
🥓 Guanciale (Guanciale Stagionato)

Cured pork jowl rubbed with sea salt, black pepper, and rosemary.

Use: Crisped in a skillet for Carbonara, Amatriciana, and Gricia.
🌿 Sweet Genovese Basil (Basilico Genovese DOP)

Tender aromatic basil leaves with subtle clove and sweet floral undertones.

Use: Pounded in marble mortars for pesto and torn fresh over hot pizza.
🏺 Aged Balsamic of Modena (Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale)

Grape must aged up to 25 years in succession barrels of oak, chestnut, and juniper.

Use: Drizzled on aged Parmigiano, strawberries, and grilled meats.

Italian Food Traditions & Feasts

La Vigilia (Feast of the Seven Fishes)

Christmas Eve (Winter)

A grand coastal banquet of 7 distinct seafood courses commemorating the eve of Christmas before midnight mass.

Staples: Baccalà, Fried Calamari, Spaghetti alle Vongole, Capitone

Pranzo della Domenica (Sunday Family Lunch)

Year-Round Weekly

The non-negotiable multi-hour family gathering where Nonna serves handmade fresh pasta and slow-braised ragù.

Staples: Handmade Tagliatelle, Ragù Bolognese, Roast Lamb, Tiramisù

Ferragosto Summer Banquet

August 15 (Midsummer)

Peak summer holiday celebrated with outdoor seaside barbecues, watermelon feasts, and crisp chilled white wines.

Staples: Insalata di Riso, Grilled Seafood, Watermelon, Vermentino Wine

Regional Italian Diversity

🧭 Northern Italy (Piedmont, Lombardy, Veneto)

Butter, polenta, carnaroli rice, rich game meats, white truffles, and Alpine cheeses.

Specialties: Risotto alla Milanese, Osso Buco, Vitello Tonnato, Polenta

🧭 Central Italy (Tuscany, Lazio, Emilia-Romagna)

Handmade egg pastas, cured pork, pecorino cheese, thick steaks, and olive oil breads.

Specialties: Carbonara, Ragù Bolognese, Bistecca alla Fiorentina, Cacio e Pepe

🧭 Southern Italy & Islands (Naples, Sicily, Puglia)

Sun-drenched tomatoes, fresh seafood, fiery red peppers, olive oil, eggplant, and citrus.

Specialties: Pizza Margherita, Pasta alla Norma, Arancini, Cannoli

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is cream forbidden in traditional Roman Carbonara?

The luxurious creaminess comes purely from the emulsion of egg yolks, rendered pork fat, starchy cooking water, and finely microplaned Pecorino Romano cheese.

What is the real difference between dry pasta and fresh egg pasta?

Dry pasta made from 100% durum wheat semolina is ideal for oil and tomato-based sauces; fresh egg pasta is delicate and porous, designed to absorb buttery meat ragùs.

Is olive oil used for everything in Italy?

In the Mediterranean South, olive oil reigns supreme; in the Alpine North (Piedmont, Lombardy), sweet unsalted butter and rendered lard are traditionally preferred.
